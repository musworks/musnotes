Who Has the Authority to Turn Growth Figures into Absolute Values? As Though |Growth| = Growth

Let us begin with a question that rarely receives much attention in economics classrooms or development meetings: who exactly grants modern economic systems the authority to treat growth figures as though they possess absolute moral value?

Within many development narratives, "|Growth| = Growth" is treated like a modern idol: a sacred entity to be accepted without much objection. When growth is announced to have risen by a certain percentage, we are seemingly expected to celebrate, without always being given the opportunity to ask: what is growing, for whom, and at what cost?

Numbers do not merely serve as instruments of measurement; they also serve as instruments of legitimacy, determining what is called progress, what is considered an obstacle, what is deemed productive, and what may be sacrificed in the name of an upward-trending graph.

My argument does not begin with the assumption that all economic growth must necessarily be harmful. What I want to question is the way growth is treated as an almost absolute sign of progress, while the ecological damage that accompanies it is often dismissed as a side effect, or merely an externality. Under this accounting system, destruction can appear as growth because the metrics are too narrow to recognise what is being lost.

As I discussed in my "previous essay" (./ekonomi%20bukan%20lawan%20ekosistem.en.md), the economy is not a system that exists outside the ecosystem. It is a derivative system that depends on the stability of the biosphere.

## What Gets Counted Becomes Real

In modern accounting systems, reality often undergoes acute distortion.

It is as though only things that can be translated into monetary values are entitled to be considered "real".

The complexity of ecosystem services — how microorganisms maintain soil health, how mycelial networks distribute nutrients, or how biodiversity sustains the stability of an area — is often treated like a ghost wandering beyond the boundaries of an economic spreadsheet.

They are unilaterally categorised as "externalities", a term that sounds technical but often serves as a subtle way of saying that something lies outside the centre of attention.

Conversely, when that same land is excavated, its ecosystem is transformed on a massive scale, and it is replaced with concrete that generates monetary transactions, it immediately becomes "real" in the eyes of the market.

We have exchanged the biological reality that sustains us for a system of value that can only recognise something once it enters a transaction.

## Relative Growth Forced into an Absolute

GDP growth is fundamentally a relative indicator.

It measures changes in the value of economic activity from one period to another. GDP growth is a comparison, a statistical movement, not an absolute measure of well-being, ecological health, or quality of life.

Yet, within mainstream development narratives, this relative measure of growth is often forced to become an absolute measure of human progress.

We are trapped in the delusion that a linear graph must continue rising indefinitely, as though the Earth possessed limitless space and resources to accommodate it.

Treating relative growth as an absolute value on a finite planet is a serious mistake. And today, we are paying the price for that mistake.

## It Seems That Forests Have No Value at All

The irony of this system becomes clear when we observe a stretch of forest.

As long as the forest stands, purifying the air, sheltering countless species, maintaining the water cycle, holding the soil in place, and supporting the local microclimate, conventional accounting often gives it little more than a passing glance before muttering: "This land needs to be put to productive use."

A living forest is considered idle and unproductive.

Then bring in the bulldozers, cut down the trees, sell the timber on the market, and transform the land into a monoculture plantation, an industrial estate, or a mining pit. Suddenly, there is economic activity to record: heavy machinery is hired, workers are paid, timber is sold, land is processed, investments flow in, and the figures begin to rise.

The economic graph can record this as an achievement. The forest is only considered useful once it has died and ceased to be a forest.

> Simply put, this system often finds it easier to value a corpse than a living thing.

## Not Everything That Grows Is Alive

Ultimately, we need to clean the lenses through which we view the world so that we can see reality more honestly.

Growth is a phase, not an endless destination. An organism grows until it reaches a certain level of maturity, after which some of its energy is redirected towards maintenance, reproduction, and homeostasis.

When a cell continues dividing without limits and ignores signals from its surrounding environment, we call it a disorder, not progress or growth.

Economic growth that recognises no limits tends to extract resources from the living systems around it, expand itself, and ultimately weaken the very foundations that make its existence possible.

We need to remember: a tree does not continue growing taller forever. At a certain point, it strengthens its trunk, expands its canopy, bears fruit, provides habitats for other organisms, and casts shade over its surroundings.

If our economic system does not know when to stop pursuing quantitative growth, it is heading towards collapse, written in the language of statistics.
